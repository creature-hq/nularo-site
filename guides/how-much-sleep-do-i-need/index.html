How much sleep do I need? And how to check you get it

Short answer

How much sleep you need, by age

The CDC publishes one table, built from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), the Sleep Research Society and the National Sleep Foundation. These are the hours of sleep recommended in a 24-hour day:

AgeRecommended sleepSource the CDC cites
0 to 3 months14 to 17 hoursNational Sleep Foundation
4 to 12 months12 to 16 hours, including napsAASM
1 to 2 years11 to 14 hours, including napsAASM
3 to 5 years10 to 13 hours, including napsAASM
6 to 12 years9 to 12 hoursAASM
13 to 17 years8 to 10 hoursAASM
18 to 60 years7 or more hoursAASM and Sleep Research Society
61 to 64 years7 to 9 hoursNational Sleep Foundation
65 and older7 to 8 hoursNational Sleep Foundation

So if you are an adult under 61, the answer to "how much sleep do I need" is seven hours or more, on a regular basis. Notice what the adult row does not say: it gives no upper limit and no magic 8. The famous eight hours is not in the guideline at all. Seven is a floor, not a target you should feel smug about hitting once.

The same numbers drive a lot of what your phone does. Your Apple Watch Sleep Score gives up to 50 of its 100 points for sleep duration, so short nights cost you there first. If you want to plan a bedtime around those hours, the sleep calculator works backwards from your alarm.

What "7 or more" really means

The adult row comes from a 2015 consensus statement by the AASM and the Sleep Research Society, written by a panel of 15 sleep experts. It is short, and three of its lines answer most of the follow-up questions people search for:

  • The floor. "Adults should sleep 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis to promote optimal health." Regularly sleeping less than 7 hours, it says, is associated with adverse health outcomes.
  • The ceiling, sort of. Regularly sleeping more than 9 hours "may be appropriate for young adults, individuals recovering from sleep debt, and individuals with illnesses." For everyone else, the panel said it is uncertain whether more than 9 hours carries any health risk.
  • The exit. "People concerned they are sleeping too little or too much should consult their healthcare provider." This page explains numbers; it cannot tell you why yours look the way they do.

Two details trip people up. First, the hours are hours of sleep. Eight hours in bed with 50 minutes awake is 7 hours 10 minutes of sleep. Second, "on a regular basis" matters. One 9-hour Saturday does not cancel five 6-hour weeknights in the way the guideline means it; the running gap between what you got and what you aimed for is what sleep debt describes.

How to check whether you get it, on Apple Watch

Most people guess their sleep from when they went to bed and when the alarm rang. That guess is usually generous. If you wear an Apple Watch to bed, the real figure is already on your phone.

1. Make sure the watch is recording. Apple says Track Sleep with Apple Watch must be on, the watch needs at least 30% charge before bed, and it has to be worn for at least an hour of the night. A dead battery means no data. If nights come back blank, how to track sleep on Apple Watch walks through the setup.

2. Read Time Asleep, not time in bed. In the Health app on iPhone, tap Browse, tap Sleep, then tap Sleep again. Tap the week tab at the top of the chart and tap each night's column. The figure to put next to the CDC table is Time Asleep. The stage chart shows the Awake segments that do not count. (If Time in Bed looks shorter than Time Asleep on older nights, here is why that happens.) On the watch itself, open the Sleep app and turn the Digital Crown to see Time Asleep for the last 14 days.

3. Set your Sleep Goal to the guideline. On the same Sleep screen, scroll to Your Schedule, tap Full Schedule & Options, and under Additional Details tap Sleep Goal. For an adult, 7 hours is the guideline floor; many people pick 7 hours 30 minutes or 8 hours for some margin. Apple says the schedule slider turns orange when a bedtime and wake-up time do not leave room for your goal, which is a quick reality check on the schedule itself.

4. Count the short nights. Look at the last seven nights and count how many fell under your goal. Three nights at 6 hours 20 minutes against a 7-hour goal is two hours behind, even if the weekly average looks close to fine. The sleep debt calculator turns a week of Time Asleep figures into one number. If you sleep in the daytime after shifts, see sleep apps for night shift workers so those sleeps are counted.

Finding your own number inside the range

"7 or more" is a population guideline. Your own need sits somewhere inside or above it, and no table can tell you exactly where. A few honest ways to narrow it down:

  • Keep a sleep diary. The CDC suggests one if you have ongoing sleep problems: bedtime, wake-ups during the night, naps, exercise, caffeine, alcohol and medicines. With an Apple Watch, the sleep half is already written; you only need to note the rest.
  • Watch how you feel against Time Asleep. Lining up a few weeks of nights against how alert you were the next day tells you more than any single night. Nights that look long but leave you wrecked are worth raising with a doctor, not solving with a gadget.
  • Use an app that estimates it. Rise says it uses algorithms and your phone use data "to work out your individual sleep need down to the minute". It is one company's model, not a measurement, and it is a subscription after the free trial. Other Apple Watch sleep apps are compared in the best sleep apps for Apple Watch.

Whatever number you settle on, the CDC's first tip is the same one that makes any number easier to hit: go to bed and get up at the same time every day, weekends included. If your bedtime has drifted, how to fix your sleep schedule sets that up in the Health app. And if you are consistently getting the hours but still always tired, or cannot get them however hard you try, that is a conversation for a doctor.

Frequently asked questions

Is 6 hours of sleep enough?

Not by the public-health guidelines for adults. The CDC lists 7 or more hours for ages 18 to 60, and the AASM and Sleep Research Society consensus says regularly sleeping less than 7 hours is associated with adverse health outcomes. If you feel you need far less or far more than the table says, talk to a doctor.

Is 9 hours of sleep too much?

The AASM and Sleep Research Society statement says regularly sleeping more than 9 hours may be appropriate for young adults, people recovering from sleep debt and people with illnesses, and that for everyone else it is uncertain whether it carries any health risk. People worried they sleep too much or too little should talk to a healthcare provider.

Do the recommended hours include time awake in bed?

No. The guidelines are about sleep. Apple Watch splits the night into Awake, REM, Core and Deep, and the figure to compare with the table is Time Asleep, not how long you were in bed.

Do naps count toward how much sleep I need?

For young children, yes: the CDC's figures for infants, toddlers and preschoolers say 'including naps'. The CDC table does not say naps count for older children, teens or adults, so the adult figure is best read as your main sleep.
