Short answer

Count back from your wake-up time in 90-minute sleep cycles, then add about 15 minutes to fall asleep. For a 7:00 AM alarm that gives bedtimes of 9:45 PM (six cycles, 9 hours), 11:15 PM (five, 7.5 hours) or 12:45 AM (four, 6 hours). Treat 90 minutes as an average: Cleveland Clinic puts one cycle at about 90 to 120 minutes. The CDC recommends 7 or more hours for adults, so five cycles is the usual floor. The math, and how far to trust it

Every sleep calculator does the same sum. Going backwards from a wake-up time: bedtime = wake-up time minus (cycles times cycle length) minus time to fall asleep. For a 7:00 AM alarm with the defaults:

69 h9:45 PM8:45 PM
57 h 30 min11:15 PM10:15 PM
46 h12:45 AM11:45 PM

The cycle part is real. The NICHD describes sleep as a loop through non-REM stages 1, 2 and 3 and then REM sleep, repeated several times a night, and says you first enter REM about 90 minutes after you fall asleep. It also says the cycles change shape as the night goes on: long stretches of deep sleep early, more REM later, and near the end almost all stage 1, stage 2 and REM.

The exact minute is not. Cleveland Clinic says one cycle normally takes about 90 to 120 minutes, and that most people go through four or five in a full eight hours. A 30-minute spread per cycle adds up fast: over five cycles, the "end of a cycle" could be anywhere in a window more than two hours wide. Your time to fall asleep moves around from night to night too. So use the result as a sensible target, not a promise that you will wake at a light moment.

Two rules carry more weight than the cycle count. The first is total sleep: the CDC recommends 7 or more hours a night for adults aged 18 to 60, 7 to 9 hours from 61 to 64, and 8 to 10 hours for teens aged 13 to 17. Four cycles is only 6 hours, which is why the calculator flags it. The second is consistency: the CDC's first tip for better sleep is going to bed and getting up at the same time every day, weekends included. A five-cycle bedtime you keep every night beats a perfect one you hit twice a week. If your bedtime has drifted, how to fix your sleep schedule covers the one-schedule setup.

Put the time into your iPhone and Apple Watch

A bedtime on a web page is easy to ignore. A schedule on your phone starts Sleep Focus and rings an alarm. To make it your regular schedule, open Health on iPhone, tap Browse, tap Sleep, then tap Sleep again. Scroll to Your Schedule, tap Full Schedule & Options, tap Edit on a schedule (or Add Schedule), and drag the bedtime and wake-up icons to the times you picked. Under Additional Details, Sleep Goal is the hours you want to spend asleep, and Apple says the slider turns orange if a schedule does not meet it.

For one late night, do not touch the full schedule. On the same Sleep screen, tap Edit under Your Schedule, drag the slider and tap Done. Apple says these Next Wake Up Only changes apply to the following day only, and your normal schedule comes back after.

Apple's Wake Up alarm rings at the clock time you set; it does not look for the end of a cycle. If you want that, it takes a third-party app. Sleep Cycle's App Store listing says its Smart Alarm wakes you during your lightest sleep phase, and our Sleep Cycle alternatives guide compares others. If what you really want is an alarm that waits until you have slept a set number of hours, that is a different feature, covered in whether Apple Watch can wake you after 8 hours of sleep.

Check the plan against what your watch recorded

This is the step every calculator skips. You planned 7 hours 30 minutes. What did you get? If you wear an Apple Watch to bed with sleep tracking on (setup is in how to track sleep on Apple Watch), the answer is already in your phone.

  • Time Asleep. In Health, tap Browse, then Sleep. Each night's bar shows when you were asleep, and the figure to compare with your plan is Time Asleep. On the watch, open the Sleep app and turn the Digital Crown: Apple says it shows Time Asleep, sleep stages and sleep duration for the last 14 days.
  • Awake time. Your plan assumed you slept straight through. The Awake segments on the stage chart are the time it did not count. The Apple Watch sleep graph explained shows how to read them.
  • The week, not the night. Tap W on the chart. If most nights land well short of the plan, the shortfall adds up, and the sleep debt calculator turns a week of Time Asleep figures into one number.

If the gap between plan and reality is big night after night, the honest answer is that a calculator cannot tell you why. If you are struggling to sleep or always tired, talk to a doctor.

Frequently asked questions

What time should I go to bed if I wake up at 6 AM?

With 90-minute cycles and 15 minutes to fall asleep, the bedtimes are 8:45 PM (six cycles, 9 hours of sleep), 10:15 PM (five cycles, 7.5 hours) or 11:45 PM (four cycles, 6 hours). The CDC recommends 7 or more hours for adults aged 18 to 60, so 10:15 PM is the latest of the three that meets it.

Is the 90-minute sleep cycle real?

Sleep does run in cycles of non-REM and REM sleep, several a night, and the NICHD says you first enter REM about 90 minutes after you fall asleep. But cycles are not all the same length. Cleveland Clinic gives about 90 to 120 minutes per cycle, so a calculator's exact minute is a rule of thumb, not a measurement.

How many sleep cycles do I need?

Cleveland Clinic says most people go through four or five cycles in a full eight hours. Five 90-minute cycles is 7.5 hours, which sits inside the CDC's 7 or more hours for adults. Four cycles is 6 hours, under that recommendation.

Can Apple Watch wake me at the end of a sleep cycle?

Apple's built-in Wake Up alarm rings at a set clock time. Some third-party apps offer a smart alarm instead; Sleep Cycle's App Store listing, for example, says its Smart Alarm wakes you during your lightest sleep phase.
