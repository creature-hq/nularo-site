Your feed has shown you the full sleepmaxxing routine: the 10 PM wind-down, the blackout setup, the morning screenshot of a 94 sleep score. You want the app part of it, the thing that holds the schedule and grades the night. Here is what your iPhone already does, what the apps called SleepMaxx actually are, and where the trend tips into worrying about a number.

What sleepmaxxing is, and which part an app can do

Sleepmaxxing is a social media label, not a method. It covers anything people do to squeeze more out of their sleep: routines, gadgets, supplements, tape, trackers. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) asked about it in its 2025 Sleep Prioritization Survey of 2,007 US adults, describing it as investing in products and strategies to optimise sleep quality. 12% said they had tried it. In the same survey, 48% of adults said they had used a sleep tracking device, up from 35% in 2023.

Split the trend into three piles and the app question gets simpler:

  • Timing. A set bedtime and wake time, every day. An app can hold this for you with a schedule, a reminder and an alarm.
  • The wind-down. A quieter phone before bed. An app (or iOS itself) can silence notifications on a timer.
  • Measuring. A score for the night and a trend over weeks. This needs a sensor, ideally a watch on your wrist.

The fourth pile, products you swallow, stick on your face or plug into the wall, is not something an app measures, and this guide does not rate them. Questions about supplements or sleep aids belong with a doctor or pharmacist, not a TikTok routine or a tracker.

The free version is already on your iPhone

Before you search the App Store, set up what Apple ships. It covers timing and the wind-down, and with an Apple Watch it covers measuring too.

A sleep schedule. Open the Health app, tap Search (Browse on some versions), then tap Sleep. Scroll to Your Schedule and tap Full Schedule & Options. Tap Edit on a schedule, drag the bedtime and alarm controls, and pick the days it applies to. You can keep separate schedules, for example one for weekdays and one for weekends. To change only tomorrow morning, edit the Next schedule instead; Apple applies that change to the following day only.

Wind Down and Sleep Focus. On the same Full Schedule & Options screen, under Additional Details, tap Wind Down and choose how long before bedtime it starts. Apple's support pages say Sleep Focus turns on at the start of the wind-down period. It simplifies the Lock Screen and holds back notifications, with exceptions for the people and apps you allow. That is the "phone in a drawer" step of most sleepmaxxing routines, done by the phone.

A nightly score. With watchOS 26 on an Apple Watch Series 6 or later, SE (2nd generation) or later, or any Ultra, each night gets a Sleep Score from 0 to 100: 50 points for duration, 30 for bedtime consistency over the last 13 nights, 20 for interruptions. Open the Sleep app on the watch in the morning and tap the Info button for the breakdown. Our guide to apps that rate your sleep compares it with AutoSleep, Bevel and Sleep Cycle, and explains why their numbers never match.

Apple asks you to charge the watch to at least 30% before bed and wear it for at least an hour of sleep. For the weekly view, open Sleep in the Health app and tap W or M at the top of the chart. The stage bands on that chart are explained in how to read the Apple Watch sleep graph.

The apps actually called SleepMaxx

Search "sleepmaxxing app" and the App Store returns several apps with nearly the same name, from unrelated developers. In November 2026 the listings included:

  • SleepMaxx: Sleep & Habit Coach (developer nooro). Its listing describes a sleep and wellness companion with smart alarm integration, 14-day sleep trends and energy insights. Full access needs a SleepMaxx Pro subscription. We could not confirm from the listing that it reads sleep from Apple Health.
  • Sleepmaxx: Sleep Score & Plan (Cambrian Labs). It scores your sleep habits from a 12-question quiz, with no wearable. That is a questionnaire about your habits, not a measurement of last night.
  • SleepMaxxing: AI Sleep Coach (a third, separate developer), whose listing mentions Apple Health integration.

Names like these change often, so treat that list as a snapshot. The useful part is the checklist. Before you start a free trial on anything with "maxx" in the name, look for three things on the listing:

  1. Does it read Apple Health? If yes, it can use the sleep your watch already records, and you log nothing. If no, it is guessing from your phone or from your answers.
  2. What does the score measure? A quiz score, a phone-on-the-mattress score and a watch-based score are three different things that happen to share a name.
  3. What is behind the paywall? Many of these apps show the score free and charge for the plan, the coaching or the history.

If what you want is the score as a game, with streaks or points, look at gamified sleep tracker apps instead. If you want the timing part graded on its own, sleep consistency apps compares how Apple, Bevel, Athlytic, Whoop and Oura define it.

When maxxing turns into orthosomnia

The trend has a known failure mode, and it has a name. In 2017, sleep researchers led by Kelly Glazer Baron published a case report in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine describing patients who were so focused on their tracker data that it got in the way of their sleep. They called it orthosomnia. The AASM now uses the same word in its survey releases for sleep lost to worrying about sleep.

A few things make a score easy to over-read:

  • The score is the app's invention. Every company weights its own inputs. Apple's score leaves out sleep stages entirely, so "more deep sleep" cannot raise it.
  • One night is noise. Watch-based stages are estimates. The weekly trend means more than any single number, and a 13-night consistency window barely moves for one late Saturday.
  • More apps, more disagreement. Two trackers on the same night will give different grades. Comparing them tells you about the apps, not your sleep.

The tracking-side version of moderation is boring: pick one score, check it once in the morning, and look at the week. If you are short several nights in a row, that is sleep debt, which Apple does not total for you. And if sleep is worrying you, or you feel exhausted no matter what any score says, talk to a doctor. No app on this page can diagnose a sleep problem, and a high score does not rule one out.

Where Nularo fits

Full disclosure: Nularo is our app, and it is closer to the opposite of a maximiser. It does not set targets you have to hit every night. It reads Apple Health read-only (sleep, sleep stages, steps, active minutes, workouts, resting heart rate, HRV, respiratory rate and SpO2), so there is nothing to log, and each morning it sends a verdict notification written from last night's numbers.

The verdict comes with a creature in one of four moods: glowing, fine-but-judgy, wrecked or suspicious. The mood is set against your own 14-day baseline, not a perfect-night ideal, so a normal night for you reads as normal. There is a Sleep score next to Stress and Energy, a 7-day sleep debt, a Bedtime Pledge you make in the evening and a Weekly Report Card for the week as a whole. If the sleepmaxxing appeal is partly the screenshot, it also makes shareable roast cards; more on that in apps that roast you.

The honest limitations: it is iPhone only, and without a watch on at night it has much thinner sleep data to judge. It reports the morning after, not in real time. It judges, it does not coach, so you will not get a sleep plan. And it is a subscription with a paywall after onboarding: $39.99 a year with a 3-day free trial, or $9.99 a month.
