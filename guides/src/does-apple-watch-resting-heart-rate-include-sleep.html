Your new ring says your resting heart rate was 52 last night. Your Apple Watch says 59 for the same day. You have not changed hearts, so one of them is wrong, or they are not counting the same thing. It is the second one, and the reason is sleep.

The short answer: no, Apple leaves sleep out

Apple's white paper on how the watch measures heart rate (November 2024) describes resting heart rate as an estimate of your lowest heart rate during periods of rest while awake. The watch takes background heart rate readings through the day when you are still, picks a subset from your most inactive stretches, and leaves out any readings taken during Sleep Focus, your scheduled Bedtime, or time the watch has detected as sleep. The result is one resting heart rate value per day.

Apple's support page says the same thing in plainer words: resting heart rate is your average heart rate while you have been inactive or relaxed for a while. Users quoting the About Resting Heart Rate text in the Health app report that it states resting heart rate does not include your heart rate while you are asleep.

So the watch still records your heart rate all night. It just files those readings somewhere else. They go into the general Heart Rate chart and into the Vitals app, not into the resting number. When you wear the watch to bed, Apple's support page notes it may even switch to infrared LEDs for those overnight readings, which is part of why the night and the day are treated as separate data.

That definition is closer to how a doctor's office thinks about it than to how most wearables do. The American Heart Association says a good time to check your resting heart rate is in the morning, after a good night's sleep and before you get out of bed, and it adds that heart rate may be lower while sleeping.

Why Oura, Whoop and Garmin show a lower number

Most other wearables build resting heart rate from your night, which is exactly the part Apple throws out.

  • Oura takes readings every 10 minutes while you sleep and reports both an average and a lowest resting heart rate for the night. The lowest one is the smallest 10-minute average it found.
  • Whoop measures resting heart rate during sleep, with more weight given to slow wave (deep) sleep, when Whoop says your body is at its most restful.
  • Garmin describes resting heart rate on its blog as your rate at rest, typically while sleeping or lying in bed after waking up. Its exact formula is not published, and owners on Garmin's forums quote more than one version of it.

A sleep-based minimum will almost always come out lower than an awake-at-rest estimate. That gap is a difference in definition, not a broken sensor. It is the same story as HRV, where Apple and the rings also use different formulas; our guide to why Apple Watch HRV looks low walks through that one.

On accuracy, the Apple number holds up well against a chest strap. A 2024 study in Sensors (O'Grady and colleagues) compared Apple Watch Series 9 and Ultra 2 with a Polar H10 in 39 healthy adults over 14 days and found an average difference in resting heart rate of 0.08 bpm. The disagreement with your ring is about what is being measured.

The practical rule: compare each device only with itself. A week of Apple numbers against another week of Apple numbers tells you something. Apple against Oura on the same morning mostly tells you they use different rules.

Where to see your resting and sleeping heart rate

Both numbers are on your iPhone. They are just on different screens.

  1. Resting heart rate (awake). Open Health, tap Browse, tap Heart, then tap Resting Heart Rate. Switch between D, W, M and 6M to see the trend. One value per day is what Apple's white paper describes.
  2. Heart rate while you slept. Go back to Heart and tap Heart Rate. Tap D and look at the stretch between your bedtime and wake time. For exact times, scroll to Options and tap Show All Data, then match the readings with the sleep times in Browse, Sleep.
  3. Overnight heart rate against your normal. Open the Vitals app on Apple Watch. Apple's support page says it shows overnight heart rate, respiratory rate, blood oxygen, wrist temperature and sleep duration, each against your own typical range. It needs Track Sleep with Apple Watch and Sleep Focus on, and about 7 nights of wearing the watch to bed before that range exists.

The overnight dots sit on top of the sleep stages, so if you want to see whether your lowest readings fall in deep sleep, our guide to reading the Apple Watch sleep graph explains each band. The same chart is where you find your Apple Watch HRV during sleep, since HRV readings land on the night the same way.

When your resting heart rate seems to include sleep anyway

Some owners in Apple Community threads say their resting number tracks their overnight number almost exactly. There are a few plain reasons that can happen, all of them about what the watch knows, not about Apple changing its definition.

  • The watch did not know you were asleep. Apple leaves out readings from Sleep Focus, scheduled Bedtime and detected sleep. If Track Sleep with Apple Watch is off, there is no sleep schedule, or the watch missed part of the night, those readings look like quiet time on the couch. Check Health, Browse, Sleep, Sleep, then Full Schedule & Options. If your sleep happens in the daytime, our guide to sleep apps for night shift workers shows how to set a daytime schedule so the watch knows.
  • Awake in bed still counts. Lying still before you get up is rest while awake, which is exactly what Apple is looking for. It is also the moment the American Heart Association picks for checking resting heart rate, so a low morning reading is the design working, not sleep leaking in.
  • The schedule and the real night disagree. If your recorded sleep and your time in bed do not line up, the edges of the night can fall either way. Our guide to Apple Watch time in bed covers why those two numbers drift apart.

None of this tells you whether a given resting heart rate is good or bad for you, and this page will not try. Your own trend over weeks is the useful reference. If your resting heart rate changes sharply and stays changed, or you feel unwell, talk to a doctor and bring the chart.

Where Nularo fits

Nularo is an iPhone app with a small creature that lives on your Apple Health data. It reads resting heart rate and HRV read-only, along with sleep, sleep stages, steps, active minutes and workouts, and you never log anything. Each morning you get a verdict notification, and the creature's mood (glowing, fine-but-judgy, wrecked or suspicious) is set against your own 14-day baseline, so it never compares your Apple number with somebody's ring.

The Overview tab puts resting heart rate and HRV next to Sleep, Stress and Energy scores and sleep debt. Resting heart rate is also one of the six habits behind Creature Age, a rules-based, for-fun number recalculated weekly; our guide to biological age apps for Apple Watch compares it with the other age scores. If you came here from a recovery app, our guide to whether Apple Watch has a recovery score shows how those apps use the same inputs.

The honest limitation: the resting heart rate Nularo uses is the one your watch writes to Health, so it carries Apple's awake-only definition and will not match a sleep-based number from another device. Without a watch there is little heart data to read. It updates the morning after, not in real time, and it judges rather than coaches, so it will not tell you what to do about a number. It is iPhone only and costs $39.99 a year with a 3-day free trial, or $9.99 a month.
