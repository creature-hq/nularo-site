The day was meetings, errands and other people's needs. Now it is 11:30, the house is finally quiet, and this is the first hour that belongs to you. You know you should sleep. You open one more episode anyway, and at 1:40 you are still awake, a little proud of the stolen time and already dreading the alarm. That has a name.

What revenge bedtime procrastination is

The research term is plain bedtime procrastination. Floor Kroese and colleagues at Utrecht University introduced it in 2014 and defined it as "failing to go to bed at the intended time, while no external circumstances prevent a person from doing so." Two parts matter. You had a bedtime in mind. And nothing was stopping you: no crying baby, no late shift, no deadline. You simply did not go.

The revenge part is newer and comes from the internet, not a lab. In 2020, journalist Daphne K. Lee translated the Chinese term 報復性熬夜 (bàofù xìng áoyè, roughly "retaliatory staying up late") as "revenge bedtime procrastination," and the phrase spread in English from there. The idea is that when the day belongs to work and obligations, the late night is the only stretch that feels like yours, so you take it back from sleep.

It helps to be clear about what it is not:

  • It is not insomnia. Bedtime procrastination is about not going to bed. If you go to bed on time and lie awake anyway, that is a different problem, and one for a doctor.
  • It is not a planned late night. Staying up for a concert you chose in advance is not procrastination. The pattern is a bedtime you meant to keep and keep missing.
  • It is not a diagnosis. It is a name for a habit, and no app or article can tell you more than that.

What the research actually found

The 2014 paper was a survey of 177 Dutch adults (people with sleep disorders and night-shift workers were left out). The authors built a nine-item Bedtime Procrastination Scale and compared scores with how people slept and how they rated their own self-control.

People who scored higher on bedtime procrastination reported fewer hours of sleep, more daytime fatigue and a stronger sense of not getting enough sleep. The link with feeling short of sleep was the strongest of the three (a correlation of 0.61). Higher scores also went with lower self-regulation (minus 0.52) and with procrastinating in general (0.60), but the overlap was not total, which is why the authors treat it as its own thing.

Two honest caveats. This is a self-report survey, so it shows things that travel together, not what causes what. And the research mostly studies bedtime procrastination in general. The "I deserve this time back" motive is the popular explanation for the revenge version, and it rings true for many people, but it is not what the 2014 study measured.

The useful takeaway is the definition itself. It is built around an intended bedtime. If you have never written one down, you cannot tell whether you are procrastinating or just running on a later clock.

See your own gap on iPhone and Apple Watch

If you sleep with an Apple Watch on, your iPhone already holds both halves of that definition. No new app needed.

Your intended bedtime. Open the Health app, tap Browse, tap Sleep (and Sleep again if asked), scroll to Your Schedule and tap Full Schedule & Options. The bedtime on your schedule is the time you told your phone you meant to sleep. If you have no schedule, setting one is step one; our guide on how to fix your sleep schedule walks through it.

When you actually fell asleep. Back on the Sleep screen, tap W at the top of the chart. Each bar starts when the watch saw you fall asleep. Our guide to reading the Apple Watch sleep graph explains the colors and gaps.

The difference. Put the two side by side for a week. One caveat: the bar starts when you fell asleep, not when you got into bed, so it always includes the time it took to drop off. A steady 20 minutes is normal. A gap that jumps to 90 minutes on quiet weeknights, when nothing was stopping you, is the pattern the research describes.

Two more places to look. On watchOS 26 or later, 30 of the Sleep Score's 100 points go to bedtime consistency, based on when you fell asleep over the last 13 nights, so a run of revenge nights costs points for about two weeks. And Settings, Screen Time shows the other side: open the activity report and switch to Day to see what the phone was doing on a late night. The watch cannot see you scrolling in bed before you drift off, as our guide to why Apple Watch time in bed is missing explains, but Screen Time can.

What people try, using what the iPhone already has

None of these is treatment. They are the settings and habits people use to make an intended bedtime harder to skip.

Start the night earlier with Wind Down. In Full Schedule & Options, tap Wind Down and pick how long before bedtime it starts. Apple says Sleep Focus turns on at the beginning of the wind-down period, so the phone goes quiet before the "my time" hour, not at lights-out.

Make the apps actually stop. On iOS 27, go to Settings, Screen Time, Screen Time Schedule, turn on Schedule, tap Add Schedule and create a late-evening window with small Time Allowances for categories like Entertainment or Social Media. Apple says apps in a category can be used until they reach that category's allowance. On iOS 26 and earlier the tool is Downtime with Block at Downtime on. Our guide to stopping doomscrolling before bed has both setups, plus the trick of letting someone else pick the passcode.

Move the revenge time earlier. If the late hour is the only free time you get, some people protect a smaller block earlier in the evening for the same show or game, so it stops competing with sleep. The point is to keep the free time and give up only the part that runs past bedtime.

Tell someone. A pledge that someone else sees is harder to break quietly. Our guide to the best free accountability buddy apps covers Apple's free options, and gamified sleep tracker apps covers games that reward leaving the phone alone.

And if the late nights are not a choice, if you go to bed on time and cannot sleep, or you are exhausted however early you turn in, stop treating it as a settings problem and talk to a doctor.

Frequently asked questions

Is revenge bedtime procrastination a sleep disorder?

No. It is a name for a behavior, not a diagnosis. The research term is bedtime procrastination, defined by Kroese and colleagues in 2014 as failing to go to bed at the intended time while no external circumstances prevent you. If you go to bed on time and still cannot sleep, that is a different problem, and one to talk to a doctor about.

Why is it called revenge?

The English phrase spread in 2020 after journalist Daphne K. Lee translated the Chinese term 報復性熬夜 (roughly, retaliatory staying up late). The idea is that when work and obligations take the whole day, the late night is the one stretch that feels like yours, so you take it back at sleep's expense.

Can my Apple Watch tell that I stayed up on purpose?

No. The watch records sleep, not why it started late. It also only counts awake time between stretches of sleep, so scrolling before you drift off is not drawn at all. What it does see is the result: a later sleep time on the chart, fewer bedtime consistency points in the Sleep Score, and a shorter night.

Is there an app for revenge bedtime procrastination?

The free tools are already on your iPhone: a sleep schedule with Wind Down, Sleep Focus, and Screen Time to block apps late at night. Apps that help with the willpower side tend to add accountability, such as a pledge, a friend, or a game that rewards leaving the phone alone.
